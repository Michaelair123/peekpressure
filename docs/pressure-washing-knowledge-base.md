# PEEK PRESSURE Field Knowledge Base
## Surface-first exterior cleaning guide
Version: 1.0  
Purpose: Internal reference for PEEK PRESSURE and a source of conservative, customer-safe guidance for Lucy.

> **Operating rule:** identify the substrate, its condition and finish, the soil, access, nearby property, and wastewater path before choosing a method. Machine maximum PSI is not a target. No pressure, nozzle, chemical, or dwell-time chart is universally safe.

This guide is a decision aid, not a substitute for equipment manuals, chemical labels/SDS, surface-manufacturer instructions, local requirements, training, or an on-site assessment. Where sources conflict, follow applicable law and the product/equipment/surface manufacturer's instructions. Do not promise stain removal or a damage-free result without inspecting the actual surface.

## 1. The job assessment sequence

1. **Define scope:** exact areas, approximate dimensions, stories/height, access, customer goals, and whether the work is residential or commercial.
2. **Identify the surface:** concrete, pavers, brick/mortar, stucco/EIFS, vinyl/fiber-cement/metal siding, painted surfaces, wood, composite, natural stone, or something unknown. Ask about age, coatings, sealers, repairs, and prior damage.
3. **Identify the soil:** loose dirt, pollen, algae/mildew, moss/lichen, oil/grease, rust, efflorescence, gum, paint, oxidation/chalking, or unknown staining. Different problems need different approaches; some are permanent or require specialty treatment.
4. **Check risk:** cracks, loose joints, flaking paint, open seams, damaged caulk, leaks, electrical fixtures, fragile glazing, plants, vehicles, pedestrians, neighboring property, and unsafe access.
5. **Plan water and runoff:** identify drains, slopes, low points, landscaped areas, and where wash water will travel. Decide whether containment, recovery, or special handling is needed before starting.
6. **Select the least aggressive effective method:** chemistry and gentle application may do more than increasing pressure. Follow product label, equipment manual, and substrate instructions.
7. **Test a representative inconspicuous area:** use the proposed process, keep within label directions, rinse appropriately, allow the surface to dry, and inspect for color change, etching, raised fibers, oxidation marks, residue, or other damage. Stop if an adverse reaction appears.
8. **Document and communicate:** photograph pre-existing defects and test results; explain limitations and any excluded areas; obtain approval for changed scope.
9. **Final inspection:** inspect after drying where practical, check for striping/residue and property impacts, and communicate remaining stains honestly.

## 2. Surface-by-surface decision guide

### Concrete: driveways, sidewalks, walkways, patios
- Often suitable for controlled mechanical cleaning when the slab is sound. A properly matched surface cleaner, steady pace, and overlapping passes can reduce wand marks.
- Concrete strength, age, finish, aggregate exposure, repairs, sealer, paint, and previous etching matter. Fresh, weak, decorative, coated, or damaged concrete needs special caution.
- Too much pressure, an unsuitable tip, holding a jet in one spot, or uneven speed can etch the surface, expose aggregate, create stripes, or damage edges.
- Organic growth, grease, oil, rust, leaf tannins, fertilizer marks, and efflorescence are not interchangeable. Some stains may lighten but remain.
- Pre-treat only with a product labeled for the surface and soil. Do not invent chemical ratios or dwell times. Do not promise full oil/rust removal.
- Check for trip hazards, loose patching, nearby doors/garages, and runoff routes. Capture/manage slurry and wastewater as required.
- **Lucy can say:** “Concrete can often be cleaned with a surface cleaner, but the finish, age and stain type affect the method and final result. We can review the area before confirming what’s achievable.”

### Interlocking pavers and paving stones
- Check joint condition, jointing sand, polymeric sand, sealer, settlement, and fragile edges before cleaning.
- Excessive force can displace joint sand, damage the surface, or worsen movement. Use a conservative test and a joint-safe method.
- Re-sanding or sealing is additional work and must not be promised unless PEEK PRESSURE has confirmed it is offered and included.
- **Lucy can say:** “Pavers need a little more care because aggressive cleaning can disturb the joint sand. We’d check their condition and any sealer before recommending a method.”

### Brick, block, and mortar
- Mortar joints and older/soft brick can be more vulnerable than the brick face. Avoid aggressive close-range jets and spraying into open or deteriorated joints.
- Check for cracks, loose mortar, previous repointing, paint, salts/efflorescence, and signs of water entry.
- Efflorescence is a salt/moisture issue, not ordinary dirt; repeated aggressive washing may not solve its cause. Acid cleaners require material-specific expertise and protection for adjacent surfaces and people.
- **Lucy can say:** “Brick and mortar vary a lot in hardness and condition, so we’d want to check the joints and any white mineral deposits before choosing a cleaning approach.”

### Stucco and EIFS
- Treat stucco and especially EIFS as moisture- and impact-sensitive until the exact system and condition are known. Prefer a compatible low-pressure/soft-wash approach where appropriate.
- Cracks, hollow or loose areas, patches, failed paint, exposed foam, and open penetrations are reasons to pause and review.
- Avoid driving water into cracks, vents, joints, or behind the finish. Do not promise that pressure washing will fix discoloration caused by oxidation, coating failure, or moisture.
- **Lucy can say:** “Stucco can be damaged by aggressive pressure, and the right approach depends on its condition and finish. We can review it before confirming the scope.”

### Vinyl, fiber-cement, aluminum, and other siding
- Identify the siding and follow its manufacturer’s cleaning instructions. Low-pressure application/rinsing is generally the safer starting point for delicate or unknown siding.
- Do not spray upward under laps or into seams, vents, soffits, window/door openings, flashing, or electrical components.
- Oxidized/chalky siding may show wipe or wand marks even without obvious physical damage. Test before broad cleaning.
- Loose panels, brittle siding, failing caulk, and damaged coatings may need repair or exclusion before washing.
- **Lucy can say:** “The method depends on the siding material, age and condition. If you’re unsure what it is or don’t have photos, that’s okay—we can use the address and arrange a review.”

### Painted surfaces and older coatings
- Cleaning can remove weak paint or reveal chalking and oxidation. Test a discreet area and do not treat paint failure as dirt.
- Buildings built before 1978 may contain lead-based paint. Do not recommend pressure washing that may disturb suspected lead paint; stop and refer the customer to a qualified lead-safe professional for assessment and compliant planning.
- Never promise paint removal, paint restoration, or a uniform finish unless the service is specifically assessed and approved.
- **Lucy can say:** “If the paint is old, chalking or peeling, washing could affect it. We’d want to assess it before recommending cleaning.”

### Wood decks, fences, and wood siding
- Wood species, grain, age, decay, moisture, coatings, and fasteners affect the safe method.
- High pressure can gouge soft grain, raise/fuzz fibers, splinter edges, and create uneven stripes. Use wood-compatible chemistry and gentle methods according to product directions; do not assume pressure alone is the answer.
- Check for rot, loose boards, protruding fasteners, peeling finish, and nearby electrical fixtures. Drying time matters before any future staining or sealing.
- Do not promise restoration of gray/weathered wood, removal of deep tannins, or a finish-ready surface without a defined scope.
- **Lucy can say:** “Wood can mark or fuzz if it’s cleaned too aggressively. We’d check the wood and finish first and set expectations for what cleaning can realistically improve.”

### Composite decking
- Composite brands and finishes differ. Follow the specific manufacturer's instructions; abrasive tools, unsuitable chemicals, or aggressive pressure can damage the cap or leave marks.
- Check warranty guidance, grease stains, mold/mildew, and whether the surface is capped or uncapped. Test first.
- **Lucy can say:** “Composite decking varies by brand and finish, so we’d check the manufacturer guidance and test the approach rather than assume it can be treated like concrete.”

### Natural stone, tile, and decorative finishes
- Identify the exact stone (for example, limestone, travertine, slate, granite), grout, sealant, and finish. Porous or acid-sensitive stone can be etched or discolored by unsuitable chemicals.
- Do not recommend acids, abrasives, or a generic pressure setting without confirming compatibility. Use a specialist where material identification or restoration is beyond the operator’s scope.
- **Lucy can say:** “Natural stone needs material-specific care. If we can identify the stone and finish, the team can assess the appropriate approach.”

### Metal, garage doors, gutters, and painted fixtures
- Coatings, corrosion, seams, electrical components, and dissimilar metals matter. Avoid forcing water behind laps, seals, vents, or electrical openings.
- Oxidation and rust may not be removable by ordinary washing; aggressive abrasion or chemicals can damage coatings.
- Do not promise gutter brightening, oxidation removal, or specialty metal restoration unless confirmed as an offered service.
- **Lucy can say:** “We can review the finish and buildup first; ordinary washing may not remove oxidation or corrosion, and aggressive cleaning can damage coatings.”

### Screens, windows, doors, and exterior fixtures
- These are vulnerable to torn screens, failed seals, water intrusion, broken glazing, and damaged electrical components. Avoid direct high-pressure spray.
- Keep spray away from outlets, lights, open windows, vents, and compromised seals. Protect or avoid delicate fixtures as appropriate.
- **Lucy can say:** “We take care around windows, screens and openings because forceful spray can cause damage or water intrusion.”

### Roofs and elevated work
- PEEK PRESSURE does **not currently offer ladder work**. Lucy must not promise roof cleaning, ladder work, or elevated access.
- Never suggest the customer climb a ladder or walk on a roof. Roof work carries fall and material-damage risks and requires suitable training, access controls, and roof-specific procedures.
- Asphalt shingles and other roofing systems have manufacturer-specific limitations; ordinary pressure washing can cause damage. Refer roof requests for an explicit scope review or decline according to current business policy.
- **Lucy can say:** “We don’t currently offer ladder work or roof cleaning. If you tell me what ground-level exterior area needs attention, I can help with that.”

## 3. Common buildup and stain expectations

| Buildup / mark | What to know | Safe customer expectation |
|---|---|---|
| Algae / mildew / green growth | Often responds to a compatible labeled cleaner and appropriate rinse; substrate and runoff protection matter. | May improve substantially; regrowth depends on shade, moisture and site conditions. |
| Moss / lichen | Can be attached firmly and may need specialized treatment; aggressive scraping/pressure can damage the substrate. | Do not promise immediate complete removal or roof treatment. |
| Oil / grease | May penetrate porous concrete; compatible degreasing and controlled recovery may be needed. | Deep or old oil stains can remain after cleaning. |
| Rust / fertilizer marks | May chemically alter or penetrate the surface; treatments can damage nearby materials. | Specialty treatment may be needed; test and do not guarantee removal. |
| Efflorescence / white salts | Often related to moisture moving salts through masonry. | Washing alone may not solve the source; investigate moisture and material first. |
| Gum | May need targeted treatment and careful removal; heat is not automatically appropriate. | Results depend on age, surface and access; don't promise zero trace. |
| Paint / graffiti | Removal is a separate specialty scope; solvents or pressure can damage substrate and coatings. | Do not quote or promise removal without assessment and confirmation that the service is offered. |
| Oxidation / chalking | Degraded pigment or coating, not simply dirt. | Cleaning can reveal or worsen unevenness; test and set expectations. |
| Concrete etching / discoloration | Physical or chemical change in the concrete itself. | Washing cannot restore material that has been etched or worn away. |
| Leaf tannins / organic stains | Can discolor porous surfaces and may need compatible specialty treatment. | May lighten without disappearing completely. |

## 4. Chemical safety
- Follow the exact product label and current SDS, including permitted surfaces, dilution, application method, PPE, ventilation, storage, and rinse requirements.
- Never mix bleach/sodium hypochlorite with acids, ammonia, or other cleaners. Never improvise chemical blends or concentrations in customer chat.
- Do not let a product dry on a surface unless its label explicitly directs that use. Dwell time is product-, soil-, temperature-, and surface-dependent; follow the label.
- Consider wind, drift, overspray, runoff, plants, pets, people, vehicles, glass, and nearby metals. Protect and rinse landscaping only in ways consistent with product directions and the site plan.
- Keep chemicals in correctly labeled containers. Do not use food or drink containers.
- If a chemical exposure or equipment injury occurs, stop work and follow the product label/SDS emergency guidance; seek emergency assistance when indicated.

## 5. Water, storm drains, and Bay Area runoff
- Inspect the drainage route before work. Do not assume wash water is harmless because the surface looks clean.
- Prevent pollutants, detergents, oils, paint debris, sediment and other contaminants from entering storm drains or waterways. Use appropriate containment, recovery, filtration, or disposal methods for the site and pollutant.
- Wastewater rules differ among municipalities and by contaminant and site. Do not claim a particular discharge or disposal method is legal without checking current local requirements.
- For San Francisco and other Bay Area jobs, verify applicable city/county stormwater and wastewater requirements before the job. Commercial sites and oily/chemical contamination may require more controls than a routine residential rinse.
- If adequate containment or lawful disposal cannot be established, pause and consult the relevant local authority or a qualified wastewater professional before proceeding.

## 6. Equipment and technique principles
- Inspect hoses, couplings, gun, trigger lock, guards, nozzles, surface-cleaner swivels, and water supply according to the equipment manual. Do not use damaged or leaking high-pressure equipment.
- Match nozzle, flow, pressure, distance, and tool to the surface and the task. Keep the nozzle moving; do not linger in one spot or aim a concentrated jet at people, animals, skin, electrical parts, or fragile surfaces.
- Maintain a stable stance and safe separation from bystanders. Secure the work zone, manage hose trip hazards, and keep customers away from the active area.
- Do not bypass guards, safety devices, unloader settings, or manufacturer restrictions. Do not diagnose or instruct repairs to high-pressure systems beyond the user's training.
- A higher-GPM machine can improve rinsing and productivity, but it does not make a fragile substrate safe for more pressure.
- Never point a pressure-washer stream at skin. High-pressure injection injuries require urgent medical evaluation even if the wound appears small.

## 7. Quoting and lead qualification for Lucy
Lucy should collect only what helps the team assess the request:
1. What area needs cleaning (driveway, sidewalk, walkway, ground-level patio, exterior wall/siding, commercial frontage)?
2. Property type and address (confirm the address before handoff).
3. Approximate size or length, and number of stories only when relevant to safe scope.
4. Surface material and condition if known; make clear that “not sure” is acceptable.
5. Visible buildup or stain type.
6. Access, water availability, nearby landscaping, drains, traffic/pedestrians, and any known damage when relevant.
7. Photos if convenient—not mandatory. If the customer declines or has none, acknowledge once and proceed with available information.
8. Name and preferred contact method/details needed by the existing lead workflow.

Do not turn this into a rigid questionnaire. Ask one useful question at a time, skip information already provided, and hand off when there is enough information. Never claim a lead was submitted unless the website workflow confirms success.

## 9. Quick decision checklist: proceed, test, or stop

**Proceed to a controlled test** only when the substrate is reasonably identified, condition is sound, access is safe, product/equipment instructions are available, and runoff can be managed.

**Pause for owner/team review** when material is unknown or fragile; paint is peeling/chalking; mortar, stucco, siding, joints, or seals are damaged; staining may be permanent; chemicals may affect adjacent surfaces; wastewater requirements are unclear; or the customer expects restoration rather than cleaning.

**Stop / decline or refer** when access is unsafe, roof/ladder work is requested outside scope, electrical or structural hazards cannot be controlled, suspected lead paint could be disturbed, equipment is defective, or lawful wastewater handling cannot be established.
